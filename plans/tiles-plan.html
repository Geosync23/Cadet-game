The rules (the whole game)

The first three are your tiles (light); the last three are your opponent's (dark, with a dashed edge). Each tile carries one of three marks: Planet ●, Ship ▲, Star ★.
  1. The board is 6 × 6. Each player starts with 12 tiles filling their two home rows. The marks are shuffled every game, but both sides always get the same set, mirrored, and no three matching tiles start touching.
  2. On your turn, do one thing:
    STEP move one of your tiles one square diagonally forward, onto an empty square (just like checkers).
    JUMP hop diagonally over an opponent's tile right next to yours, forwards or backwards, into the empty square behind it. You take that tile into your hold: it's yours now, keeping its mark.
    LAUNCH put a tile from your hold onto any empty square in your two home rows.
  3. DOCK After every step, jump or launch: if the tile you just moved, landed or launched touches two or more of your tiles with the same mark, edge to edge, in any shape (a line, an L, a block), the whole group docks: it lifts off the board and scores 1 point per tile. Three touching scores 3; four scores 4.
  4. Winning: the first to 7 points wins. If neither does in 14 turns each, the higher score wins. A tie goes to whoever docked last (and if nobody docked at all, to whoever has more tiles). If the player whose turn it is can't move, the game ends there and is scored the same way (higher score, then whoever docked last).

That's all of it. You move like checkers, you score like a matching game, and taking your opponent's tiles is how you get the marks you need.

Why it's different

The one twist: you capture to collect, and you match to score. In checkers, a capture removes a piece. Here a jump takes the tile into your hold, mark and all, so you jump to collect the mark you need, then launch it to finish a match. In match-3 games, tiles fall from nowhere; here every tile you match was moved, jumped for or launched on purpose, and your opponent can see it coming and break it up.

  • Moving and matching pull against each other. You step diagonally, but you match edge to edge, so lining up a group takes a little planning.
  • Scoring costs you. Docked tiles leave the board. The leader thins out; the player behind keeps more tiles to jump with. That's the built-in catch-up.
  • Every turn is one tap-tap decision from three verbs: step, jump, launch. No chains, no forced moves.

Balance and tests

Before writing this page I wrote a small throwaway simulator and played thousands of games between computer players at three strengths (the same three the crew will use; see "Crew opponents"). Results for the rules above:

  • First-player advantage: none worth fixing: 49–51% at every real skill level. Moving first means you reach the middle first, which also means you're the first to be jumped, and that evens it out. The mirrored deal means neither side gets better marks. If real play shows a lean, the lever is ready (below).
  • The tie rule matters more than it looks: level scores, settled by "whoever docked last", decide 10–24% of games, and the second player wins more of them (Medium: 177 to 107 in 2,000 games), because the 14-turn cap ends on the second player's turn. The first player makes up for it by winning more of the decided games, so the totals stay at 49–51%. tiles_check.py will report tie-break wins by seat so we can watch it.
  • No runaway leader: docking removes your own tiles, so the leader gets weaker on the board. The player behind at the halfway mark still wins 37–40% of the time.
  • Comeback route: a jump both takes a tile from the leader and gives you a mark to launch; a 4-tile dock scores 4 in one go.
  • Draws are rare: 0–0.1% between real players.
  • Short: about 9–11 turns each. At around 8 seconds a turn for you and 2 for the crew member, that's about 2–3 real minutes; in ship time we set it to about 10 ship minutes. 14 turns each is the hard cap (about 4 real minutes), and about half of strong games end on 7 points before that.
  • Skill matters, but not too much: Hard beats Medium 68% of the time, Medium beats Easy 70%, Hard beats Easy 83%. A new player can beat a good one now and then.
  • No stronger "champion" level: I tried one that looks a move further ahead. It was no better than Hard (50% against Hard, 55% against Medium), so Rina plays at Hard. A Medium-strength player beats her about 32% of the time, so a decent player beats her about one game in three.
  • All three verbs get used: a typical game has 3–4 docks, 2–4 jumps and 1–3 launches.

The simulator was a throwaway; when the game is built, tiles_check.py plays the same few thousand games against the real code and checks these numbers stay in range (first player 48–52%, draws under 1%, average game under 24 turns) and reports how many games the tie rule decided, by seat. The simulator is kept next to this page as plans/tiles-sim.py.

Learning it in the first game

  • First game: the teaching layout (the board above) against an Easy opponent. One move is shown: a pointer on your first possible jump, with one line: "Jump their tile to take it. It's yours now." After that, you just play.
  • A three-line card sits under the board the whole time: STEP · JUMP · LAUNCH, and "3 of a mark touching = DOCK". Tap it to hide it.
  • Tap a tile and its legal squares glow. A square that would make a dock shows a small spark. You never have to work out what's allowed.
  • The opponent talks a little: Tomas might say "Ooh, you could've docked there" after a missed dock (once a game at most).

Accessibility

Colour-blind safeEvery mark is a shape (circle, triangle, star) and a colour. Whose tile it is shows as solid light versus hollow dark with a dashed edge, a brightness difference, not a colour. It still reads on the one-colour phosphor screen.
Big tap targetsOn a phone the board fills the screen width: each square is about 56 points wide, and never under 44 (the usual minimum): on the smallest 320-point phones the board is 6 × 44 = 264 points, which fits inside the device screen with the a–f and 1–6 labels in a thin margin outside it. Tap a tile, then tap where it goes. No dragging. Tap the tile again to cancel.
No timers, no reflexesTake as long as you like. The ship keeps running around you, but the board waits. Your needs keep ticking slowly, as they do when you sit anywhere.
One-handedThe board sits low on the screen, in thumb reach, with your hold in a row just under it. Everything is a single tap.
Calm motionEffects are short and follow the device's existing "reduced motion" setting: with it on, tiles simply appear in place.
Words tooEach move also writes one short line ("Rina jumped your Ship on c4"), so nothing happens only as a picture.

The look

  • On the device's screen. When you sit at the station or a lounge table (the play pose), the board opens in the device's old-school phosphor screen, with its bezel, scan lines and your chosen phosphor colour. Your opponent sits across the table in the 3D world, and the lounge carries on behind.
  • Tiles are rounded squares like little hull plates; the marks are a Planet, a Ship and a Star. In full colour they are teal, coral and gold; on the phosphor screen they take its tint.
  • Small, satisfying effects: a jump is a short arc hop and the taken tile slides into your hold; a dock flashes the group, lifts it with a little ring of light, and the score ticks up; a 4-tile dock gets a double ring.
  • Phone budget: the board is drawn by the device's screen (plain page elements and simple CSS movement), so it adds no 3D draw calls. The 3D boards on the tables belong to the deck architect (one shared mesh for every lounge table, at most 3 draw calls).

How a crew opponent plays

Crew use three strengths, each a simple rule, plus a little personality on top.

LevelThe rule
EasyHalf the time any legal move; otherwise the Medium choice.
MediumThe best move right now: a dock if there is one (the bigger the better), else a jump, else anything.
HardMedium, but it also looks at your best reply and avoids leaving a tile you could jump or a group you could break.

Personality is a couple of small leanings each person has (seeded from who they are, so the same person always plays the same way): bold (likes jumps), patient (waits to dock four rather than three), chatty (talks during the game), and a line or two at the end.

Rina: plays to winHard, bold. Quiet during the game; a dry line when she docks ("That's three."). When she wins: "Good game. Again?" She defends her title every Tiles night.Beat her and it's news: "Rina lost! To the new hand!" A big bump in social, and she remembers it.
Tomas: plays to chatEasy, patient and very chatty. Talks about his day between moves, points out a dock you missed, cheers your good moves. He's the one who teaches you.Win or lose, a bit of friendship and a line of chatter afterwards.

You can't lose anything. A game gives a little social with your opponent (more if you win, a little even if you lose), a line of chatter, and a Record line for your first win and for beating Rina.

Tiles night (Tuesday 20:00, the bow lounge)

  • Crew play each other on paper. At the start of the night, the crew who come are paired (seeded by the day, so the same save always gives the same night). Each pairing is decided by a seeded roll using the strengths above (Hard beats Medium about 68% of the time, and so on), so no real games are played in the background and it costs nothing on a phone.
  • You can join: sit at any free board and someone sits down to play you; or challenge Rina at the station.
  • Results become news the next morning: "Rina won again (her ninth Tuesday running)", "Tomas finally beat Okafor", "The new hand took two games".
  • Rina's streak is part of her arc. On the board she plays at Hard; on paper the rolls would break her streak about one Tuesday in three. My suggestion: her Tiles-night results are a story rule, not a roll (she wins her crew games until you beat her, or until her arc says otherwise). See the questions below.

Rule ideas tried and rejected

All tested in the same simulator before settling on the rules above.

"Shatter": match anyone's tilesThree of a mark in a line clear whoever owns them; you score for the opponent's tiles in it.Rejected: matches almost never happened (about 2 a game), so it played like plain checkers, and games ran 45–60 turns.
"Flip": jumped tiles change sidesLike Othello: a tile you jump turns to your colour where it stands.Rejected: tiles flipped back and forth endlessly (up to 30 flips a game) and 40–80% of games ended in draws.
"Any mark": three of yours in a rowMarks don't matter; any three of your tiles in a line dock.Rejected: far too easy. Games were over in 9–11 turns, almost nobody jumped, and the first player won 52–55%.
"Head start" for the second playerThe second player begins with one tile in the hold.Not needed for balance, and too strong as a fix: the second player then won 63%. Kept in the pocket as a handicap if you'd like an option to give a new player a boost.

Questions for you

  3. Two people on one phone: the brief says "with 2 people". In the ship it's you against crew. Would you also like a pass-the-phone mode at the station for two real people?
  4. The prize: social, chatter and a Record line, as above, or something you can keep (a small trophy on your bunk shelf after beating Rina)?
  5. Length: first to 7 points (about 2–3 real minutes). Or would you like a shorter "quick game" to 5 as well?
  6. Name: just "Tiles", as the ship calls it, or something with a bit more character ("Dock Tiles", "Wrenstones")?
