Controller Support Plan

Playing Starship Cadet with a game controller: an Xbox, PlayStation or Switch-style pad, or a controller your phone clips into. Everything the game asks of you, done without touching the screen. Studied on build 0.64.14 (claude/integration). Nothing has been built yet; your answers at the bottom decide the details.
The one hard rule. With no controller connected, the game looks and plays exactly as it does today, for touch and mouse players alike: the same pictures pixel for pixel, the same saves, the same crew. Even with the feature switched on, nothing new is drawn, added to the page or saved until a controller's button is actually pressed. Every turn proves it with the regression test (ALL IDENTICAL against the build before).

What you'd get

  • Walk with the left stick, turn and zoom the view with the right stick and shoulder buttons.
  • A soft ring on the nearest thing you can use (a person, a seat, a machine, a door, the lift), with a small line at the bottom: A Talk to Rina. Press A and it does exactly what tapping it does.
  • Every panel, card and menu (your device, the lift, conversations, the job board, Tiles, Lightdisc, the Pause card, Dev mode) moves a highlight with the D-pad, presses with A, and goes back with B.
  • Small button pictures next to things you can press, drawn in your controller's own style, shown only while you're using the controller. Touch the screen and they vanish at once.
  • A Controller page in your device's Settings: change any button, walking and camera sensitivity, button pictures, swap confirm and back.

How the game takes input today (what the plan builds on)

On the deck (section 11): a tap runs tap(x, y), which finds what's under your finger and does the right thing (talk, sit, use, the lift, a door, walk there). Press and hold sets player.steer, a direction and a speed, and you walk toward your finger. Pinch and the mouse wheel zoom; the Rotate button turns the view a quarter turn.

Panels and menus are ordinary page buttons: the device (section 19 draws every app the same way), the lift's deck list, the sleep alarm, conversations (#chat), the Tracked list, the job board and Ship health, the crew store, the ladder, work-step cards (choose and turn), the Pause card, the career and sick-day cards, the arrival's name box, and Dev mode's sheet. Most already close with the Escape key, and the lift and Pause card already move keyboard focus.

The mini-games: Tiles is a 6 by 6 grid of buttons inside your device. Lightdisc has its own full-screen view and already reads arrow keys into a move value.

So the controller needs no new way of doing anything: it steers the way a held finger does, presses the same buttons, and calls the same tap code. That's why it can stay invisible to everyone else.

2. Picking things on the deck

With touch, you point at anything on screen. A controller has no finger, so the game picks for you: the nearest thing you can use within about three tiles, in the direction you face first, gets a soft ring at its feet and a line at the bottom of the screen naming it (A Sit, A Talk to Holt, A Lift). D-pad left and right step through the other things nearby, nearest first. The list is the game's own list of things you can tap, so nothing new has to be marked up on any deck.

Pressing A runs the very same tap code at that thing, so every special case already built (the ladder behind glass, a sealed door's text, the lift roof job, a job stop seen through glass, seats and treadmills) works without a second copy. To get somewhere far, you walk with the stick, or pick a place in Tracked and follow the goal arrow as you do now.

The ring is one small shape, drawn only while you're using the controller (one extra draw call, well inside every deck's budget).

3. Panels and menus without touching the screen

One small helper knows the game's panels and which one is on top (the Pause card over everything, then Lightdisc, the career and sick-day cards, the question card, the lift, the sleep alarm, the ladder, the crew store, the clock screen, Tracked, your device, a conversation, the job board, Dev mode). When one opens, the highlight goes to its first sensible button (the lift already does this: the deck your goal is on). The D-pad moves to the nearest button in that direction, as on a TV menu. A presses it, exactly like a tap. B does what Escape or tapping outside does for that panel.

The highlight is a clear outline in the game's teal, drawn only while the controller is in use. Screen readers hear each button's name as the highlight lands on it, because it's the page's real focus.

The buttons on the screen's edge (Pause, Rotate, Shadows, Walls, Crew, New game, the needs strip, Device, Tracked) are reached with D-pad up from the deck; B goes back to walking. New game still needs its two presses.

Typing (your name on arrival, a memo, Dev mode's search): highlighting the box and pressing A brings up your phone's own keyboard. See question 6 for a controller without a phone keyboard.

5. Button pictures on screen

Small button marks in the controller's own style: letters for Xbox, the four shapes for PlayStation, letters in Nintendo's places for a Switch pad, worked out from the name the controller gives the browser (you can pick one in Settings). They sit beside the things they press: on the Device button (Y), Pause (Menu), Tracked (View), Repair or Hobby (X), Lightdisc's Leave (B), and on the bottom line naming the ringed thing.

They appear only after a controller button is pressed, and go the moment you touch the screen or use the mouse, so a phone player who picks the controller up and puts it down sees the screen go back to exactly how it was. They follow the text size setting and never cover speech bubbles (they sit on the HUD's buttons, not over the deck).

6. Connecting and disconnecting mid-game

  • Connecting: browsers only reveal a controller after its first button press (a privacy rule). That press shows a short message, once per session: "Controller connected. Press A to use the highlighted thing." Nothing else changes until then.
  • Disconnecting (battery, Bluetooth, unclipping the phone): you stop walking at once, and if you'd used the controller in the last half minute the game pauses with the usual Pause card, so the ship doesn't run on while you reconnect. The Pause card's line reads "Controller disconnected" in that case. Touch keeps working throughout.
  • Reconnecting: any button brings the highlight back; Start or A on the Pause card resumes.
  • Two controllers: whichever pressed a button last is the one in charge.
  • Switching back and forth: touch, mouse and controller all work at the same time. Whatever you used last decides whether the marks and highlight show.

7. Settings: changing buttons and sensitivity

A Controller page in your device's Settings app, shown only once a controller has been seen (so the Settings app is unchanged for everyone else):

  • Change a button: each action in a list; highlight one, press A, then press the button you want. A button already used elsewhere swaps places. "Back to normal" resets them all.
  • Swap confirm and back (Nintendo style, B to confirm) in one switch.
  • Walking: how far the stick moves before you walk (dead zone), and full speed or gentle.
  • Camera: zoom speed, flip zoom up and down, and turning by flick (one quarter turn per push) or by holding (repeats).
  • Button pictures: automatic, Xbox, PlayStation, Nintendo, or off.
  • Vibration: a light buzz when you pick something up or finish a job (off unless you turn it on; question 4).

Saved with your game, and only when you change something from normal, so a save without a controller stays byte for byte the same (about 100 bytes when you do).

8. Accessibility

  • Nothing needs a button held down or pressed fast: walking stops when you let go, and every other action is one press. Holding a direction in a menu repeats at a calm pace (a pause of half a second, then four steps a second; both adjustable).
  • Every action can be moved to any button, which also covers adaptive controllers (the Xbox Adaptive Controller shows up as an ordinary pad), and one-handed play by putting the main actions on one side.
  • The highlight is a thick, high-contrast outline, and the ring on the deck pulses gently unless your phone asks for reduced motion, when it stays still.
  • The highlight is the page's real focus, so screen readers read each button and panel by its existing name.
  • Vibration is never the only sign of anything.

9. How it stays invisible to touch and mouse players

  • All of it sits behind one switch, PAD_ON, off until a reviewed switch-on turn (the project's rule for new features).
  • With the switch on and no controller: no new page elements, no new styles, no new saves, no new random numbers. The only thing added is a listener that waits for a controller to say hello, and one quick "is there a controller?" check in the main loop that does nothing until one has.
  • The controller never invents behaviour: it steers like a held finger, presses buttons like a tap, and taps the deck through the existing tap(). The crew's planner never sees it.
  • The new code lives in its own section (section 26, CONTROLLER), with one-line hooks elsewhere: the main loop's poll, Lightdisc's stick, and the Pause card's line. Each owner reviews their hook.

10. Testing without a real controller

A new check, controller_check.py, puts a pretend controller into the test browser: before the game loads it replaces the browser's controller list with one scripted pad (buttons and sticks the check sets frame by frame) and announces it the way a real one does. The check then plays the game with it:

  1. Nothing changes without a pad: cadet_test ALL IDENTICAL against the build before, with the switch off and on; and with the pretend pad plugged in but never pressed.
  2. Touch after pad: press a button, then tap the screen: the screenshot matches the no-pad one exactly.
  3. Walking in all four view directions: pushing up walks up the screen every time; letting go stops.
  4. Turning and zooming both ways, with the same limits as pinch.
  5. Picking: by a crew member, a seat, a machine, a sealed door and the lift, the ringed thing is the expected one, and A gives the same result as the regression test's tap on it.
  6. Every panel reachable: for each panel in the list it opens it, then walks the highlight in all four directions from the first button and checks every visible button can be reached, pressed, and the panel closed with B. This is the "nothing needs the screen" proof.
  7. A whole day by pad: wake, eat, ride the lift, talk, do a job step, sleep, using only the pretend controller.
  8. Tiles played to the end, and a Lightdisc game started, moved and left.
  9. Disconnecting mid-walk pauses and stops; reconnecting resumes.
  10. Settings: change a button, save, reload, the change holds; a save without changes is byte-identical.
  11. A controller that doesn't report the standard layout, and two at once.
  12. Draw calls and frame time with a pad in use, at phone size.

Plus the usual: check_fast before every push, deck, device and interactions checks for the hooks, and names_check. A real controller test on your phone (question 7) before switch-on, because some phone browsers handle controllers their own way.

12. Risks

  • The Claude app on your phone may not pass controllers to the game. Phone apps that show web pages don't always support controllers, and some phone controllers send "Back" as a phone button that can leave the page. Turn 1's tester page finds out on your phone in a minute, before the big turns. If it doesn't work there, controllers still work in a phone or computer browser.
  • Picking the wrong thing in a crowded mess hall. Facing direction comes first and D-pad steps through the rest; the check tests crowded rooms.
  • Panels other sessions add later won't be in the panel list. The reachability check fails on any open panel it doesn't know, so it gets added in the same turn.
  • Merge clashes: almost all the work is in its own new section; the hooks in other sections are one line each and go past their owners.
  • A pad that doesn't report the usual layout (some Switch pads in some browsers): a best-guess layout plus the change-a-button page.
  • Speed: reading a controller costs almost nothing, but the ring and the highlight are checked for draw calls and frame time at phone size.

Questions for you

QUESTION 1 · CONFIRM AND BACK ON A SWITCH PAD
On a Nintendo pad, the confirm button is on the right, not the bottom. Which should confirm?
One rule for every pad; Nintendo players can swap in one press.
QUESTION 2 · CONTROLLER DISCONNECTS
If the controller drops out while you're playing, should the game pause?
The ship doesn't run on while you reconnect.
QUESTION 3 · GETTING AROUND
Should there be a button that walks you to your goal (like tapping far away does), or do you walk there with the stick?
Closest to how touch plays now.
QUESTION 4 · VIBRATION
A light buzz for picking things up, finishing a job, a goal reached?
QUESTION 5 · THE KEYBOARD
The same highlight could also work with a keyboard's arrow keys and Enter. Add that too?
It would change how the keyboard behaves today, and the rule for this work is that nothing changes without a controller.
QUESTION 6 · TYPING WITH NO PHONE KEYBOARD
On a computer or TV with only a controller, typing your name or a memo needs a keyboard. What then?
QUESTION 7 · YOUR CONTROLLER
Turn 1 makes a tiny tester page: open it in the Claude app with your controller, press some buttons, and it shows whether the game can see them. Will you try it before the big turns?
It's the biggest unknown, and it takes a minute.
QUESTION 8 · REVIEW
Who checks the work before it's merged?
Catches problems early; one session more while it runs.
